# Improving Access to Diverse Foods and Economic Well-Being

## Hero Summary

CeRID is implementing a three-year food security and livelihoods project in Dollow District to help vulnerable internally displaced and host-community households increase food production, improve dietary diversity, build sustainable income, and strengthen their resilience to drought, flooding, displacement, and market shocks.

The project combines agroecological farming, access to seeds and tools, rehabilitation of irrigation canals and rangelands, vocational training for women, Village Savings and Loan Associations, cash-for-work, and household gender dialogue.

**Project status:** Ongoing  
**Implementation period:** March 2025 – February 2028  
**Location:** Dollow District, Gedo Region, Jubbaland State, Somalia  
**Implementing organisation:** CeRID  
**Member organisation:** Trócaire  
**Funding partner:** Canadian Foodgrains Bank  
**Project code:** SOM166

> The figures presented below are selected achievements from the project’s first reporting year, covering March 2025 to February 2026. They are not final end-of-project results.

---

## Project Overview

Food security in Dollow remains fragile due to recurring drought, failed rains, extreme heat, flooding, livestock losses, displacement, high food prices, insecurity, and disruption to trade and transport.

These pressures have reduced agricultural production, weakened pastoral livelihoods, limited household income, and increased dependence on harmful coping strategies. Women-headed households, internally displaced families, small-scale farmers, and pastoral communities face especially serious barriers to food, productive resources, financial services, and sustainable livelihoods.

The project responds through an integrated approach that links food production, climate adaptation, productive infrastructure, women’s livelihoods, savings, financial literacy, and gender-transformative household engagement.

---

## Overall Goal

To improve vulnerable households’ access to diverse and nutritious food while strengthening economic well-being, climate resilience, and equitable participation in household and community decision-making.

---

## Key Objectives

1. Increase sustainable food production through agroecological farming.
2. Improve farmers’ access to quality seeds, tools, knowledge, and local agricultural markets.
3. Rehabilitate irrigation canals and degraded rangelands.
4. Expand income opportunities for internally displaced and host-community women.
5. Improve women’s access to savings, loans, business skills, and productive assets.
6. Promote shared household decision-making and gender equality.
7. Strengthen community resilience to drought, flooding, and other climate-related shocks.
8. Improve accountability, local participation, coordination, and learning.

---

## Who the Project Supports

The project works with vulnerable people across 14 villages and IDP settlements in Dollow District, including:

- Small-scale host-community farmers
- Lead farmers
- Agro-dealers and local seed suppliers
- Internally displaced women
- Women-led households
- Pastoral households
- Cash-for-work participants
- Women operating or planning small businesses
- Village Savings and Loan Association members
- Couples participating in household gender discussions

The intervention areas include Dayah, Unaa, Surguduud, Qootay, Dhuma Dhumy, Garaash, Quracley, Khilawo, Bantaal Bay, Qurdubay, Maskiinow, Hila Shied, Kulanle, and other targeted communities in Dollow District.

# What the Project Does

## 1. Agroecology Training for Lead Farmers

The project trains lead farmers in practical agroecological methods so they can demonstrate sustainable farming and accompany other farmers in their communities.

Training topics include:

- Land preparation
- Soil-health management
- Organic manure production
- Ecological pest control
- Water conservation
- Crop diversity
- Recycling organic matter
- Harvesting techniques
- Seed sourcing
- Seed banking
- Farm layout and design

During Year 1, 80 lead farmers participated, including 20 women and 60 men.

Lead farmers use practical demonstrations and peer learning to transfer knowledge to other vulnerable farming households.

---

## 2. Community-Based Farmer Training

The 80 lead farmers trained and accompanied 620 vulnerable farmers in agroecological practices.

The cascading approach enables farmers to receive locally relevant support from people within their own communities. Practical exercises help participants apply new techniques directly on their farms.

Post-training assessments showed strong improvements in knowledge related to:

- Soil fertility
- Water harvesting
- Land preparation
- Pest and disease management
- Organic inputs
- Recommended weeding practices
- Farm layout
- Post-harvest practices

Seed banking remained an area requiring further training and reinforcement.

---

## 3. Seeds, Tools, and Agricultural Inputs

The project supported 700 farmers through a conditional voucher system that provided seeds and basic farming tools.

Inputs included:

### Seeds

- Treated maize
- Cowpeas
- Sorghum
- Spinach
- Green pepper
- Watermelon

### Tools

- Forks
- Hoes
- Spades

Five competitively selected agro-dealers delivered the inputs directly to villages, reducing transport barriers for farmers.

Post-distribution monitoring found that:

- All sampled farmers received the correct inputs on time.
- 99% rated the quality and quantity as good or very good.
- 95% planted the seeds received.
- All respondents retained their tools for continued use.

The small quantity of unplanted seed consisted mainly of vegetables considered too risky under severe heat and expected rainfall failure.

---

## 4. Supporting Local Agro-Dealers

CeRID trains agro-dealers and producers in:

- Local seed production
- Seed quality assurance
- Storage
- Seed security
- Post-harvest handling
- Marketing
- Market linkage
- Agribusiness development

Twenty agro-dealers and producers participated during Year 1. All scored above the project’s post-training knowledge benchmark.

Strengthening agro-dealers helps improve farmers’ future access to appropriate, high-quality seed through local markets rather than relying only on external distributions.

---

## 5. Crop Production and Household Food Security

Despite severe drought and unpredictable rainfall, supported households produced maize, cowpeas, sorghum, spinach, and fodder.

A Year 1 harvest analysis sampled 250 of the 700 supported farmers. The findings showed:

- 77.2% harvested a highly diverse combination of crops.
- 16.5% harvested a moderate range.
- 6.3% harvested a more limited crop mix.
- 43% of harvested produce was used for household consumption.
- 57% was sold to generate income.

The sampled farmers collectively reported substantial quantities of grain, vegetables, and fodder. The produce supported household food consumption, livestock feeding, and market sales.

These results demonstrate the value of combining agricultural inputs with farmer training, crop diversification, and locally appropriate agroecological practices.

---

## 6. Irrigation Canal Rehabilitation

The project engaged 232 internally displaced participants—93 women and 139 men—to rehabilitate riverine irrigation canals through cash-for-work.

Year 1 activities restored **5,000 metres of irrigation canals**.

The rehabilitation included:

- Clearing blocked sections
- Removing sediment and vegetation
- Reshaping canal structures
- Improving water flow
- Restoring access to irrigation
- Strengthening community management of water infrastructure

Each participant worked for five days and earned USD 35. A total of USD 8,120 was distributed to participating households.

The restored canals now provide more reliable water access for host-community farmers and help strengthen agricultural resilience.

---

## 7. Rangeland Rehabilitation

The project engaged 300 pastoral community members—120 women and 180 men—in rehabilitating degraded rangelands through cash-for-work.

Activities included:

- Clearing invasive bushes
- Opening and reshaping soil bunds
- Constructing water-retention structures
- Reducing soil erosion
- Improving communal grazing areas

The project completed physical rehabilitation across **150 hectares** and established 900 semi-circular bunds.

Each participant worked for five days and earned USD 35, resulting in USD 10,500 distributed to households.

The physical works were completed, although failed rainfall limited vegetation regeneration during the reporting year. The structures remain in place to support recovery when rainfall improves.

---

## 8. Vocational Training for Displaced Women

The project enrolled 40 internally displaced women from Kabasa, Ladan, Kansahley, and Kaharey camps in six months of vocational training.

Training areas included:

- Tailoring
- Beauty salon services
- Henna
- Business management
- Bookkeeping
- Pricing
- Customer care
- Risk management

Of the 40 graduates:

- 28 completed tailoring training.
- 12 completed beauty and salon training.

All participants graduated and received start-up kits to establish small businesses.

The women were referred through Trócaire-supported nutrition facilities, linking emergency nutrition support with longer-term livelihood recovery.

---

## 9. Women’s Enterprise Start-Up Support

Graduates received equipment and materials suited to their chosen businesses.

The support enabled women to begin providing locally demanded services such as:

- Garment cutting and stitching
- School uniforms
- Somali women’s clothing
- Household textiles
- Hair care
- Henna
- Basic facial treatments

Monitoring found that all 40 women received their kits in the correct quantity and quality, and all began income-generating activities.

Reported earnings included:

- Beauty salon graduates serving an average of two customers per day at around USD 3 each.
- Higher earnings of approximately USD 18–20 during weddings and Eid.
- Tailoring graduates earning approximately USD 5–7 per day.

These are early reported earnings from Year 1 and should not be presented as guaranteed or permanent income.

---

## 10. Village Savings and Loan Associations

The project trained 50 women from IDP and host communities in Village Savings and Loan Association methodology.

Training covered:

- Group formation
- Leadership and elections
- Group constitutions
- Savings procedures
- Social funds
- Loan conditions
- Record keeping
- Repayment
- Share-out procedures

Two groups were formed:

- Towfiq Group
- Tanad Group

Each group has 25 members.

Participants received start-up kits including passbooks, stamps, cash boxes, registers, padlocks, notebooks, and mobile phones with SIM cards.

Monitoring found that:

- 49 of 50 women demonstrated strong knowledge of VSLA methodology.
- Members saved USD 5 per month.
- Three members accessed loans totalling USD 200.
- Loans were used for banana production, kiosk restocking, and healthcare.
- USD 80 had already been repaid during the reporting period.

---

## 11. Gender Discussion Groups

The project facilitated household-level gender discussions with 180 participants—90 women and 90 men.

Sessions covered:

- Household budgeting
- Communication
- Shared responsibilities
- Conflict resolution
- Negotiation
- Household income and expenditure
- Gender roles
- Joint decision-making

The sessions used role-playing, practical budgeting exercises, and household scenarios.

Participants reported:

- Greater recognition of shared household responsibilities
- Improved communication
- Increased involvement of women in financial decisions
- Greater interest in monthly family meetings
- Better understanding of joint household planning

The project also learned that women often communicate more openly in women-only spaces. Future implementation will continue combining joint dialogue with safe, separate opportunities for women to express their views.

---

## 12. Peer Learning and Exchange

The project organised peer exchange sessions for 90 women from IDP communities, livelihood groups, and VSLAs.

Participants exchanged practical experiences on:

- Household nutrition
- Dietary diversity
- Food safety
- Locally available foods
- Home gardening
- Savings
- Loan repayment
- Small-business management
- Group rules and governance

The sessions strengthened mutual learning and encouraged participants to apply successful approaches from other women and communities.

- Farmers are gaining practical knowledge of agroecological production.
- Households are producing a more diverse mix of crops.
- Agricultural inputs are improving access to food and marketable surplus.
- Fodder production is helping households support livestock.
- Rehabilitated canals are improving irrigation access.
- Cash-for-work is creating income while restoring productive community assets.
- Women are establishing tailoring and beauty businesses.
- Savings groups are helping women access small loans and manage collective funds.
- Women are increasing their participation in household financial planning.
- Men are showing greater recognition of shared decision-making.

# Gender Equality and Women’s Empowerment

Gender-sensitive design is integrated across the project.

Key measures include:

- Flexible working hours for women in cash-for-work
- Shorter hours for pregnant women and women caring for children
- Safe transport for vocational trainees
- Separate consultations and women-only discussion spaces
- Equal access to farming inputs and training
- Women’s leadership among lead farmers
- Women-led savings groups
- Targeted vocational and business resources
- Household gender discussions involving spouses
- Support for women’s role in budgeting and decision-making

Women represented 596 of the project’s 1,432 direct participants during the reporting year.

# Environmental Stewardship

The project conducted a locally led environmental assessment using tools provided through Trócaire and the Caritas Interagency Group on Disaster Risk Reduction and Resilience.

The assessment found no significant negative project effects on:

- Water sources
- Soil stability
- Vegetation
- Waste generation
- Hazardous substances
- Cultural or archaeological sites
- Land tenure
- Ecologically sensitive zones

Two risks require continued management:

## Climate-Related Hazards

Drought, extreme heat, flooding, and unreliable rainfall continue to threaten food production and restoration work.

**Response:** Agroecology, drought-tolerant seeds, irrigation canals, water harvesting, soil restoration, crop diversification, and rangeland rehabilitation.

## Water-Related and Vector-Borne Disease

Seasonal water stagnation and proximity to riverine areas may increase malaria and other disease risks.

**Response:** Continued coordination with health and environmental actors and integration of public-health risk awareness into field monitoring.

# Accountability and Community Feedback

CeRID uses a Community Complaints and Response Mechanism to collect feedback through:

- Phone calls
- Community meetings
- Project committees
- Face-to-face communication
- Suggestion boxes
- Field monitoring

During Year 1, 41 non-sensitive feedback cases were recorded and addressed.

Common issues included:

- Need for more tools
- Requests for refresher training
- Need for literacy support
- Training hours
- Cash-for-work wage expectations
- Delays or questions about start-up kits
- Need for women’s workspaces

Project responses included:

- Reducing daily training hours
- Integrating basic literacy support
- Clarifying tool and kit timelines
- Strengthening extension support
- Providing immediate cash-for-work payment
- Improving training facilities
- Planning further follow-up in Year 2

The need for safe and accessible workspaces for women’s enterprises remains an area for continued action.

# Challenges and Adaptive Responses

## Severe Drought and Failed Rainfall

Drought reduced water availability, limited crop performance, and prevented expected rangeland regeneration.

**Response:** Farmers received drought-tolerant seed, agroecology training, crop diversification support, fodder production guidance, and improved irrigation access.

## Flooding and Climate Variability

Flooding and unpredictable seasons affected farming, irrigation, and local livelihoods.

**Response:** Riverine canals were rehabilitated, water-retention structures were constructed, and implementation plans remained flexible.

## Tool Shortages

Cash-for-work participants reported insufficient tools for canal and rangeland activities.

**Response:** Communities shared available tools during Year 1. Provision of basic tools is prioritised for the next implementation cycle.

## Women’s Time and Mobility Constraints

Pregnant women, mothers, and displaced participants faced challenges attending long training sessions.

**Response:** Training hours were reduced, schedules became more flexible, and pick-up and drop-off transport was provided.

## Limited Business Workspace

Some women operate businesses from sleeping areas or small household spaces.

**Response:** The project plans to explore shared workstations near women’s homes and link graduates more closely with VSLA financing.

## Weak Seed-Banking Knowledge

Knowledge assessments showed that seed banking remained weaker than other agricultural topics.

**Response:** Refresher training and continued technical accompaniment will give greater attention to seed storage and community seed systems.
